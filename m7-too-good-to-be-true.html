Too Good to Be True

In the Kaggle competition, a model can look better and better on the houses it learned from and still get worse on the houses that count. In this lab, you add predictors until the fit looks excellent, chase the leaderboard, meet a feature that is too good to be true, and then review the decisions that an AI tool made for you.

This lab takes about 20 minutes and also works on a phone. Nothing is graded or saved.

1More predictors, better fit?

Steps 1 to 4 use a synthetic town modeled on the Kaggle data: 400 training houses with known prices and 400 hidden test houses whose prices the models never see, just as in the competition. Every model in this lab predicts the log of the sale price (step 2 explains why), and its score is the RMSE of the log prices (which the course calls RMSLE), so lower is better.

Please move the slider to add predictors one at a time, first the ten house features and then 25 extra columns. The extra columns are random numbers: I generated 1,000 of them and kept the 25 that happened to fit the training houses best, which is what any automatic search for “the best predictors” does when it can choose from many columns (the Kaggle file has 79).

2Why the log?

Sale prices are right-skewed, i.e. most houses sell for moderate prices and a few sell for much more §2.5.4 · p. 50. Taking the log pulls in the long right tail, which is one of the transformations Novikov describes for skewed variables Novikov · p. 17.

Training houses

Kaggle’s score works on the log scale, so it counts percentage errors. On the log scale, a difference is (approximately) a percent change, which is the same idea that the book uses for price elasticity §5.5.5 · p. 218. Please pick a house price below; the error is always $20,000.

In many Kaggle competitions, the leaderboard that you see during the competition (the public leaderboard) scores each submission on only part of the test houses, and the final ranking uses the other part (the private leaderboard). The book asks leaders to require a leakage check whenever results look “too good to be true”, since leakage inflates performance during testing and then collapses in production §6.7.6 · p. 316. Novikov gives the same warning: make sure that no future information, such as the outcome itself, makes its way into the training data Novikov · p. 45.
