Bending the Line

A subscription company has run 200 weeks of pricing experiments in three product categories. Please fit a line through its demand data, find out where the line bends, and then work out in which category a price increase would actually raise revenue.

This lab takes about 20 minutes and also works on a phone. Nothing is graded or saved.

1A straight line through a curve

The data in this lab are modeled on the book's pricing case, i.e. 200 weeks of pricing experiments for a consumer subscription product in three product categories, with weekly units sold as the outcome §5.1.1 · p. 197. Each dot below is one week. As in the book, we start with the simplest model, a straight line of units on price, as a first sanity check §5.4.7 · p. 207.

The lower panel then shows each week's miss (actual minus line) and the average miss in five price bands.

2Percent changes: the log-log model

Pricing decisions are usually discussed in percent ("a 5% increase") rather than in dollars, which is why the book's pricing case uses a log-log model, i.e. a straight line of log(units) on log(price) §5.5.5 · p. 218. In this model the slope is the price elasticity, the percent change in units that goes with a 1% change in price. Moreover, weekly units are right-skewed (), and taking logs is a common way to make such a variable easier to model Novikov · p. 17. Please switch the chart to the log scale.

The shortcut is the one in the book's example, where an elasticity of about −1.2 and a 5% price increase give roughly 6% fewer units §5.5.5 · p. 219. It works well for small changes.

3Back to units

The log-log model predicts log(units), but nobody plans production or revenue in log units. To get back to units, you exponentiate the prediction, so that units = epredicted log(units). This is also how the book turns its log-log model into demand and revenue predictions for candidate prices §5.8.6 · p. 241. Please pick a price.

4Three categories

So far one line has described all 200 weeks, although the three categories differ in their baseline demand. The book's pricing model therefore adds category indicators (0/1 columns), so that the price effect is not mixed up with differences between categories §5.9.5 · p. 247. One category is left out and becomes the baseline; each indicator coefficient is then the difference from that baseline at the same price. Novikov calls these columns one-hot encoding, with one 0/1 column for every category Novikov · pp. 16–17. In a regression with an intercept, one of them has to be left out, since the three columns always add up to the intercept column. Please choose the baseline and watch which numbers change.

5Different slopes

The parallel lines of step 4 assume that customers in every category react to price in the same way. The book's diagnostic question for this case is exactly whether the elasticity is stable across product categories §5.7.5 · p. 233, and a scatterplot by category is the first check for such grouped structure §5.3.2 · p. 201. To give each category its own slope, we add the interaction of category and log(price), as we did with Far × Salary in the lecture Lecture 3a · slides 13–17. Please switch between one slope and three slopes.
